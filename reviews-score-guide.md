# How to Improve Your Reviews & Reputation Score

Reviews & Reputation is the highest-weighted category in [Neerzy's free GBP audit](https://www.neerzy.com/gmb-audit-tool) — 25% of your total score, tied with Completeness. If your overall audit score came back lower than you'd like and Reviews & Reputation was the weak category, this page is the deep dive on fixing exactly that.

## What This Score Actually Measures

The audit checks four things under this category:

- **Total review count** — how many reviews you've accumulated
- **Average rating** — your overall star rating
- **Review velocity** — how many new reviews you've received recently, not just historically
- **Response rate and time** — whether, and how quickly, you respond to reviews

It's worth noticing what's *not* in that list: the audit doesn't score review length, doesn't require a specific star rating floor to pass, and doesn't penalize a single negative review. It's measuring an ongoing pattern of activity and trust, not a perfect record.

## Why Velocity Matters More Than Most Plumbers Think

Two plumbing businesses can have the identical total review count and average rating and still score very differently here, because of one factor: recency. A business with 80 reviews, all from three years ago, reads to Google very differently than a business with 80 reviews and five new ones in the last month. Google is trying to answer "is this business still good, right now" — and a stale review pile, however positive, answers a slightly different question than the one being asked.

This is why the single highest-leverage fix for a low Reviews & Reputation score usually isn't chasing a higher star rating — it's re-establishing a steady monthly flow of new reviews, even a modest one.

## How Many New Reviews Per Month Is "Enough"?

There's no universal number, since it depends on your local market's competitiveness, but a reasonable target for most small plumbing businesses is **4–8 new reviews per month** — roughly one for every few completed jobs. Businesses in dense, competitive metro markets should aim higher; businesses in smaller towns with less local competition can often maintain a strong score with fewer.

## The Request: Timing, Wording, and Common Mistakes

**Timing.** Ask immediately after the job is completed — not the next day, not "when things settle down." Satisfaction peaks the moment the problem is solved and fades measurably within 24–48 hours.

**Wording.** Short and specific beats long and polished:

> "Hi [Name], thanks for choosing [Business]. Would you mind leaving a quick review?"

Paired with a direct, one-tap link to your Google review page. Longer, more formal requests consistently see lower click-through — the extra words are friction, not persuasion.

**Common mistakes that suppress response rate:**
- Asking too many days after the job, when the memory has faded
- Burying the review link behind a "click here" instead of a direct one-tap URL
- Sending the exact same generic message to every customer, with no name or technician reference
- Offering a discount or incentive in exchange for a review — this violates Google's policies and puts your entire profile at risk if flagged

## Responding to Reviews (Both Directions)

Response rate and time are scored directly, which means responding is not optional politeness — it's a measured input.

**For positive reviews:** a brief, genuine thank-you that references something specific from the review reads as authentic and takes under a minute.

**For negative reviews:** acknowledge the issue without getting defensive, briefly state what you did or will do about it, and take further detail offline. Future customers reading the exchange are judging your response to the problem far more than the problem itself — a thoughtful reply to criticism often builds more trust than it costs.

## What a Strong Reviews & Reputation Score Looks Like in Practice

As a rough benchmark: businesses scoring well in this category typically maintain 50+ total reviews, a rating at or above 4.5 stars, at least a handful of new reviews every month, and a response rate close to 100% within 48 hours. None of these individually guarantees a top score, but together they represent the pattern the category is built to detect.

## Fix This, Then Re-Check

Review-based improvements move more slowly than photo or post-based ones, since they depend on real customers actually leaving reviews over time — expect to see measurable score movement over 4–8 weeks of consistent requesting, not overnight.

**A realistic four-week starting sequence:**

- **Week 1:** Respond to every existing unanswered review, positive and negative.
- **Week 2:** Start sending a review request after every completed job, using the short template above.
- **Week 3:** Keep the requests going — this is the week most businesses quietly stop, which is exactly why it matters most.
- **Week 4:** Re-run the audit and compare against your starting score.

## Common Questions

**What if someone leaves a one-star review?**
Respond professionally and briefly, without getting defensive, and take further detail offline — see the response guidance above. A single low review, especially one you've responded to well, rarely has an outsized effect on a category built around ongoing patterns.

**Should I try to get a fake review removed, or ask friends to leave reviews?**
You can flag genuinely fake or policy-violating reviews for Google to review. Asking friends or family who aren't real customers to leave reviews violates Google's policies and risks your entire profile — it's not worth the risk for a small, short-term bump.

**Can I buy reviews?**
No — purchased reviews violate Google's terms, are increasingly detected and removed in bulk (sometimes taking legitimate reviews with them), and can result in profile suspension.

**Should my employees leave reviews for the business?**
No — reviews from employees, even well-intentioned ones, violate Google's conflict-of-interest policies and can be removed if identified.

**How many reviews are actually enough?**
There's no fixed finish line — competitive markets generally need more to stay visible, but the more useful target is an ongoing monthly flow rather than a one-time total to hit and stop at.
